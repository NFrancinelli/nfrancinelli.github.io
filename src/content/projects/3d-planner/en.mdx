## Why this project

Most open-source navigation for ROS 2 is built for ground robots. Nav2 plans on a 2D costmap, so a drone using it has to fly at a fixed height like a ground robot that happens to be in the air. That limitation stopped my [VI-SLAM project](/projects/vi-slam/) at mapping: the next step, flying planned trajectories through the map, needed a planner that works in 3D.

This project fills that gap. I wrote a 3D path planner from scratch that lets a PX4 drone fly to a goal through space it has never seen. Everything around it is off-the-shelf: Gazebo Harmonic, PX4 SITL, OctoMap and ROS 2 Jazzy, all in Docker.

## Results: how much to trust unseen space

The drone only knows what its depth camera has seen, so when the best route runs through unmapped space, the planner has to decide whether to risk it. A* allows unknown voxels at an extra cost per metre, `unknown_cost`. The benchmarks showed there is no best value. Each setting is a bet, and whether it pays off depends on the hall:

- in a hall where the unseen way is a dead end, the cautious setting (10) beat the default by 27 s;
- in a hall where the unseen way is a shortcut, it lost by 15 s;
- the default (1) takes the shortcut when there is one, and turns back to the known route once it sees a dead end.

Every benchmark is a two-leg mission, flown 3 times for each value of `unknown_cost` (0, 1 and 10) at half real-time speed. All 18 legs in each hall reached their goal. The closest any run came to an obstacle was 0.41 m, for a drone radius of 0.39 m. Times below are averages of the 3 runs.

**Open hall.** The return leg can use the corridor mapped on the way out or an unexplored aisle of about the same length, so the cost barely matters: 34.6 s at 0, 36.1 s at 1 and 35.8 s at 10. The difference shows on the outward leg, where the drone at cost 0 is surprised more often (13.7 blocked replans against 8.0 at cost 1).

Caution also costs computation. Higher values make A* expand much more of the hall: up to about 170k expansions and 0.4 s per search at cost 10, against about 9k and 25 ms at cost 0.

## How the planner works

The planner runs A\* on a live OctoMap, built from one forward depth camera (320×240 at 10 Hz, 10 m range, pitched up 12°). Obstacles are inflated and unknown voxels cost extra per metre. Climbing and passing close to obstacles cost extra too, so paths keep their altitude and run down the middle of gaps.

I shortcut the A\* path to a few waypoints and fit a piecewise quintic through them. The result ramps up to max speed, rounds corners within 0.75 m and is collision-checked against the map with the drone's radius. A separate trajectory server streams its position, velocity and acceleration setpoints to PX4 in offboard mode.

The map keeps growing during the flight, so the plan keeps changing. On every map update (5 Hz), the planner checks the rest of the trajectory and replaces it if it hits a newly seen obstacle. Every 2 s it also takes a clearly faster plan if it finds one, meaning at least 1 s and 10 % faster. A new plan takes over 1 s ahead on the current trajectory and matches its position, velocity, acceleration and heading, so the drone never jumps. When it's stuck, the drone brakes to a hover. It brakes immediately if an obstacle would come within 0.4 m before a new plan could take over.

I worked out the speed limit from the stopping distance. At 1 m/s and 1 m/s², the drone needs about 2.0 m to stop after an obstacle appears: 0.75 s of reaction (about 0.9 m), 0.94 m of braking and up to 0.18 m of tracking error. The camera's 10 m range leaves plenty of margin. Occluded corners don't: in the factory, a face comes into view about 2.5 m away. Along the way I measured that PX4 overshoots the commanded speed by about 16 % per m/s² of acceleration, and worked out why.

The camera never sees beside or below the drone, so the planner blocks unknown space within 1.5 m of a plan's start. I call this the known-space rule.

For debugging, every plan can be dumped and replayed offline with `plan_replay`, and `mission_metrics.py` measures each flight.

In the `factory` world, the flight from the start to behind a crate wall takes about ten replans as the hall comes into view. The drone discovers the dead end, climbs over the crates and stops within 15 cm of the goal, in about 40 s.

Localization uses simulated motion capture on purpose: it isolates the planner from state-estimation error. Flying on onboard estimation, such as visual-inertial SLAM, is the natural next step.

## What the benchmarks found

Four problems only showed up once I started measuring flights, and fixing them changed the design:

- The planner could freeze mid-flight. Braking while the drone was still speeding up searched forever for a duration that fit, and the drone flew its last trajectory blind into the crate wall. Braking now accounts for the starting acceleration, and every duration search is bounded.
- Low-cost plans turned into the unseen sides of shelving blocks, because the camera can't see sideways: 4 of 6 runs at cost 0 grazed or hit one. That's where the known-space rule comes from.
- The drone crept down long aisles. The hall has no roof, and OctoMap only maps along rays that hit something, so the space straight ahead stayed unknown. The camera's no-return pixels now become rays that clear space up to 10 m.
- OctoMap punched holes in walls seen at a grazing angle. Occupied voxels are now sticky, which is safe because the worlds are static.

## Limitations and next steps

- Localization is simulated motion capture, so robustness to onboard estimation error isn't evaluated yet.
- There is a single forward depth camera. The test worlds have no obstacles directly above or below the flight path, and the walls beside the drone go unseen.
- It runs in simulation only, in static worlds: obstacles never disappear from the map.
- Max speed is 1 m/s, limited by occluded corners.
